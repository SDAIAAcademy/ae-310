AE-303 treats traces as the raw material of evaluation; here the same traces are the raw material of training. Ask: which of the four lines would you train on? The request and the correction. Qayd's own wrong answer is never a training target, but it tells you which behaviour to look for in other traces.

Counts from Lab 2.1 on the supplied 500 traces. Selection bias is the quiet problem of trace-based data: what gets labelled depends on what people bothered to fix. The dataset card says so in its limits.

The first trap

"Accepted" means nobody objected, not that the call was right

8 of 342
accepted traces carry a wrong call (the generator's answer key)
  • A rule check finds 2 of them: the number in the call is not in the request
  • The other 6 pass every check: valid, plausible, wrong
  • In your own project there is no answer key: only an audit of a sample tells you the rate

The eight are planted. Three quarters of the examples from traces are accepted ones, so their error rate matters more than the corrections'. Label noise of two or three percent rarely stops a model from learning; it does put a ceiling on what the test can show, and it teaches the specific wrong decisions that were accepted.

Lab 2.1 · task 3

The tool's own schema is the first reviewer of every label

for trace in traces:
    call = labels[trace["id"]]          # Qayd's output, or the desk's call
    if call is None:
        continue                        # abandoned: no label
    errors = C.schema_errors(call)      # what the registry would refuse
    if errors:
        rejected.append((trace, call, errors))
    else:
        kept.append((trace, call))

C.schema_errors checks the tool name, the required arguments, their types, the lists of allowed values and the shape of a registration number. It is the same function the grader uses, which is the point: training labels and evaluation share one definition of a valid call.

Session 2

Teacher generation and verifier filtering

  • Generate labels with a larger model under frozen instructions
  • Filter teacher outputs with schema checks and rule checks
  • Measure yield and estimate the errors a verifier lets through
02
Labels for what the desk never saw

A larger model can label requests nobody corrected

  • The pilot left 900 requests with no label at all
  • On Day 1 the larger model was right on 94 in 100 with the frozen instructions
  • Its labels are cheap, fast and in volume, where staff time is none of the three

The price

It is wrong on about 6 in 100, and whatever enters the set is what the student learns. A teacher needs a verifier.

This is data distillation: the student learns from the teacher's outputs. Session 3.4 returns to it as a method; today it is a data source like the other two, with its own error profile. The stand-in teacher's error rate was written into the script; a real teacher's must be measured.

Two conditions before you call the teacher

Same output format as the student, and only data that may leave

Format

The teacher gets the frozen instructions and examples. Its label must be the exact text the student has to produce, or the student learns someone else's format.

Placement

The pool in the lab is synthetic. Real members' requests are Restricted: a hosted teacher may not see them. Run the teacher on your own servers, or label masked and synthetic requests only.

The teacher's terms count too. Its licence and its provider's conditions on training another model with its outputs belong in yesterday's licence table.

The placement card is the responsible-use point of the session and it is easy to skip in a lab where everything is synthetic. Say it plainly: the requirement that made the chamber choose a small model also forbids sending the real traces to a hosted teacher.

Lab 2.2 · task 3

A rule check compares the call with the text it claims to answer

numbers = CR.findall(C.western_digits(text))   # numbers the request contains
args = call["arguments"]
if "cr_number" in args and args["cr_number"] not in numbers:
    errors.append("the registration number is not in the request")
if call["name"] == "escalate_to_staff" and args.get("reason") == "missing_cr" and numbers:
    errors.append("escalated as missing although the request gives a number")
if call["name"] == "create_membership" and args.get("company_name", "") not in text:
    errors.append("the company name is not in the request")

Each rule encodes one sentence a registry officer would say. Write rules for what must be true of every right answer, and test each rule for false alarms: a rule that rejects right answers silently removes a kind of example. The stretch shows one such rule.

The audit is the lesson. Zero in thirty feels like proof and is not: the Wilson upper bound is 11.4%. The true rate here is 2.7%, known only because the data is synthetic. 03
What the model actually reads

The model never sees your messages: it sees one string

<|im_start|>user
نأمل إرسال شهادة العضوية للسجل التجاري 4029184372.<|im_end|>
<|im_start|>assistant
<tool_call>
{"name": "issue_certificate", "arguments": {"cr_number": "4029184372", "kind": "membership"}}
</tool_call><|im_end|>

Role markers, an end-of-turn token, a wrapper for the call. Every one of these is a convention of this model, written in its template.

Printed in Lab 2.3, task 1. The template is a small Jinja program stored with the tokeniser. Transformers applies it with apply_chat_template; a server applies the same file. If participants did the pre-work they have seen the template of a real model and can say where its tool calls go.

Sources: Transformers documentation, chat templates.

The durable idea

The chat template is part of the model

  • The base model was trained on strings in exactly this shape
  • Your adapter will be served strings in exactly this shape
  • So train on strings in exactly this shape: use the template, do not rewrite it

What goes wrong

A hand-written renderer trains "fine": the loss falls, because the model learns whatever text it is given. The fault shows in production, as a format nobody sends.

A falling loss is evidence that training ran, never that the text was right. This is why the lab compares strings character by character before any training step.

Two renderings of one example

The server sends the prompt; training adds the answer

Prompt

add_generation_prompt=True: the request, then the marker that opens the assistant's turn. The model writes what follows.

Full

The whole conversation, the assistant's turn and its end-of-turn token included. The full text must start with the prompt, character for character.

That prefix property is what makes the label mask possible: everything up to the end of the prompt is context, everything after it is the target.

The lab asserts full.startswith(prompt). When that assertion fails with a real model, the usual cause is a template that inserts something different with and without the generation prompt, for instance a reasoning block. Then the mask must be built from the template's own output, not from string lengths.

Change the base model and the tuning set must be rendered again. Store messages, never rendered strings.

Both forms are from the models' own cards, read on 15 November 2026. The same tags, different content: a parser written for one silently fails on the other. This is why yesterday's examples were stored as messages with tool_calls.

Lab 2.3

Three faults that train "fine" and fail in service

1 · The turn never ends

No end-of-turn token after the answer. The adapter never learns to stop and writes to the token limit on every request.

2 · A foreign call format

str(call): single quotes, no tags. The model is taught the Python-dictionary failure of Lab 1.1.

3 · Loss on everything

Labels equal to the input. A third of the signal goes into predicting members' requests instead of the answer.

All three are in the colleague's renderer in the lab, and all three are common in real projects. Fault 1 also raises cost: a model that does not stop bills the maximum output on every request. Fault 3 on the lab's example: 82 tokens, 56 of them in the answer; unmasked, 32% of the loss is spent on the request.

Fault 3, fixed

The loss is taken on the assistant's turn only

prompt_ids = tok(prompt, add_special_tokens=False)["input_ids"]
full_ids   = tok(full,   add_special_tokens=False)["input_ids"]
labels = [-100] * len(prompt_ids) + full_ids[len(prompt_ids):]
#         ignored by the loss       the tool call and the end of turn
Why it matters beyond efficiency. A model trained to predict requests is trained to reproduce what members wrote. Masking the prompt is also a data-protection measure.

-100 is the index PyTorch's cross-entropy ignores. Libraries offer this as an option (training on completions only); know whether yours does it, and check one example by decoding the supervised tokens, as the lab does. add_special_tokens=False because the template already wrote every special token: a tokeniser that adds its own start token would double it.

The check people skip

Encode, decode, compare: does the text survive the tokeniser?

writtenالرد على owner289@member.example أو الاتصال على 0509022407.
decodedالرد على owner289<|unk|>member.example أو الاتصال على 0509022407.

The course tokeniser has no token for the @ sign. Masking removes the address before the tokeniser sees it (Lab 2.4), and the lab's fixed renderer round-trips 50 of 50 masked examples.

A real finding from building this course: the first version of the lab rendered unmasked examples and some of them failed this check. With a real model's tokeniser the usual culprits are normalised characters, stripped spaces before special tokens and unknown symbols in a small vocabulary. Run the check on every example before the first training step.

Checkpoint

It answers, and then it keeps writing

A freshly tuned adapter produces the correct tool call and then continues with more calls and stray text until the token limit. The base model never did this. Where do you look first?

The rendered training text: is there an end-of-turn token after the assistant's answer?
The learning rate: it was too high
The server: raise the stop-token limit
The tuning set: it needs more examples
Why. A model stops because it was shown stopping. If the training text never ends the turn, no setting teaches it to.

Answer: A. Demonstration if time allows: print the last ten tokens of one rendered training example. If the end-of-turn token is not among them, the diagnosis is done.

Session 4

Deduplication, decontamination and personal data

  • Remove near-duplicates with a stated rule and threshold
  • Report overlap with the test set, separating leaks from template collisions

04
What a loss curve will never show

Duplicates inflate, leaks flatter, personal data does not belong in weights

Near-duplicates

The same request, sent twice with "Hello" in front. The set looks larger than it is, and one member's wording is over-weighted.

Test-set overlap

A test request in the tuning set. Every score on Day 4 would be too high, by an unknown amount.

Personal data

Names, mobile numbers, identity numbers in free text. Not needed to choose a tool, and a model can reproduce what it was trained on.

None of the three raises an error or bends a curve. They are found only by looking for them, which is why outcome 2 lists each by name and asks for a report.

A rule you can state in one sentence

Near-duplicate: one text's three-word runs are contained in the other's

keptKindly extend the membership of CR 4013583134 by 1 year.
removedKindly extend the membership of CR 4013583134 by 1 year. Regards.
  • Normalise: lower case, Western digits, no punctuation
  • Compare runs of three words; containment = shared runs divided by the smaller set
  • Near-duplicate at 0.9 or more, compared only within the same registration number

Containment rather than plain overlap, because a re-sent request is the original plus a greeting: the smaller text is wholly inside the larger. The lab removes 67 near-duplicates from 746 rows. Embedding similarity is the heavier alternative; start with a rule you can explain to the data protection officer.

A threshold is a decision, and it has a cost

At 0.8 the rule merged two different requests

kept at 0.8نرجو إرسال قائمة بجميع الأعضاء العاملين في الاستشارات.
removed at 0.8نرجو إرسال قائمة بجميع الأعضاء العاملين في الأجهزة الطبية.

Two short requests that differ only in the activity they ask about: five of the six word runs of the shorter one are shared. At 0.8 they count as one; at 0.9 both stay. The lower threshold removed 82 rows, the higher one 67.

Read what a threshold removes before you accept it. Print ten removed pairs; if one is not a duplicate, the threshold is wrong for your data.

This happened while the course was being built: the first threshold was 0.8 and the first removed pair printed was this one. Short texts are the weak point of any shingle rule, because one changed word is a large share of a short text. The report states the rule and the threshold so that a reader can disagree.
